What Carolina has to do against the No. 3 Irish

Overview

There is no hiding from what this game is.

North Carolina is 2-1. Notre Dame is 4-0, ranked No. 3, and has scored 169 points while allowing just 33 through four games. UNC has scored 70 and allowed 41 through three. The Irish are coming off a 49-10 win at Purdue, while the Tar Heels have spent two weeks sitting with the Clemson loss, a game they led 17-6 at halftime and 20-15 in the fourth quarter before inexplicably losing 28-20.

This is also the first meeting between the two programs since 2022, when Notre Dame won 45-32 at Kenan Stadium. The Irish lead the all-time series 21-2.

On the surface, this looks like a game where UNC has to play close to perfectly. The numbers explain why.

Notre Dame ranks near the top of the country in almost every efficiency metric, as the table below shows. The gap that matters most, though, opens up in obvious passing situations.

Notre Dame’s offence has a 59.9% success rate on standard downs, fifth nationally, but just 23.2% on passing downs. Carolina’s defence has been excellent in passing situations, allowing a 22.7% success rate (26th nationally), but it is allowing 47.1% on standard downs. Notre Dame has been very good at avoiding the situations where a defence can dictate play, and that is probably the biggest theme of this game: UNC has to make the Irish play behind the chains.

If the Irish are constantly sitting in second-and-six or third-and-two, pressure from the Tar Heel front becomes much harder to create. If UNC can consistently push them into third-and-seven or third-and-eight, the matchup changes.

That is the path Carolina has.

This is the biggest statistical gap on the field.

Billy Edwards Jr. has completed 44 of 78 passes (56.4%) for 611 yards, three touchdowns and one interception, with a 132.3 passer rating and 7.8 yards per attempt.

CJ Carr has completed 67 of 95 (70.5%) for 963 yards, nine touchdowns and one interception, averaging 10.1 yards per attempt with a 184.8 passer rating.

The gap goes beyond the box score. Notre Dame’s passing EPA per play is +0.570, 11th nationally in the current CollegeFootballRatings data, against +0.148 (94th) for UNC.

The FBS-only Game on Paper snapshot, which excludes FCS opponents, makes the gap even more obvious: Carr is at +0.50 EPA and 9.59 yards per dropback, Edwards at -0.10 EPA and 5.34.

For Carolina, the answer cannot be trying to turn Billy into Carr for one afternoon. UNC must make his job easier.

Against Clemson, Edwards completed just 13 of 30 passes for 135 yards, one touchdown and one interception. The run game carried the offence, but once Clemson loaded the box and forced obvious passing situations, the passing game struggled to keep drives moving. That is exactly where this Notre Dame defence wants the game to go.

Some of the blame lies with his receivers, as outside of Shipp no one stepped up to help. But Billy also needed to plant his feet and steady himself before many throws, and his inability to do so led to a number of wayward passes.

Edwards does have one important trait the raw rushing numbers do not capture: he can create positive plays with his legs. FBS-only tracking credits him with 61 rushing yards on seven carries (8.71 per carry, 0.56 EPA per rush). Sacks are handled differently in the official NCAA rushing totals, which is why that looks so different from his official season line.

That matters because the Fighting Irish are not going to make life easy for him in the pocket, with Bryce Young emerging as a second major pass-rushing threat alongside Boubacar Traore.

The Clemson game also showed why moving Billy can be useful. He does not need to become a designed-run quarterback; a few well-timed boots, scrambles and pocket movements could turn a third-and-eight into a third-and-three. That is very different from asking him to throw the ball 40 times.

Benjamin Hall has been UNC’s best offensive player through three games, and he projects to carry the largest workload against ND. He has 215 rushing yards on 33 carries (6.5 per carry), including 18 carries for 130 yards against Clemson, where UNC ran for a season-high 214. Demon June, mostly used as a goal-line powerback, has 115 yards on 25 carries, while Jaylen McGill, UNC’s best receiving and pass-blocking back, has 86 on 13.

The offensive line deserves credit too. UNC’s 4.02 offensive line yards per carry ranks second nationally, and its 58% run-play success rate is fifth.

The problem is what they are running into. Notre Dame’s defence has allowed a 28% rushing success rate and -0.229 rushing EPA per play, is giving up just 2.33 line yards per carry, and has a front-seven havoc rate of 17%, fifth nationally. This is probably the clearest strength-vs-strength matchup on the field.

The concern in the running back room has been fumbling. Hall fumbled in UNC’s half during the TCU game, which brought June in for the final offensive drive, and June fumbled against Clemson on the play after a back-breaking interception that should have broken Clemson’s spirit.

Abandoning the run would be a mistake, and so would running into Notre Dame’s strength on every early down. The offence should stay committed to Hall while using formation, motion, misdirection and the passing game to stop the Irish stacking the interior.

Hall does not need 150 yards for Carolina to have success. If he consistently creates four- to six-yard runs, forces the Irish to respect play-action and keeps UNC out of third-and-long, he has done his job. That matters especially because Notre Dame’s rushing offence is far less intimidating than its passing game.

Aneyas Williams has 224 yards on 52 carries and Nolan James Jr. has 197 on 46, both averaging 4.3 yards per carry, with seven touchdowns and 421 yards between them. Jonaz Walton has been the explosive change-of-pace option, averaging 6.8 yards on his 16 attempts.

The raw numbers can be misleading here. Notre Dame’s rushing offence averages only around 4.1 yards per carry, but it has a 53.1% rushing success rate, ninth nationally. The Irish consistently create functional runs, and that is enough. UNC cannot let them turn those runs into easy first downs.

The defensive line has been one of UNC’s biggest improvements this season. Xavier Lewis has 17 tackles, 8.5 tackles for loss and six sacks (two against Clemson), Jaylen Harvey has two sacks and Leroy Jackson has one. That group’s most important job is not piling up sacks but stopping Notre Dame from staying ahead of schedule, and the possible return of Melkart Abou-Jaoude would help there tremendously.

This is probably where Notre Dame creates its biggest problem for Carolina’s secondary. Micah Gilbert has 334 receiving yards and two touchdowns on 15 catches, Jordan Faison has 239 yards and two touchdowns, and Jaden Greathouse, Jerome Bettis Jr., James Flanigan, Cooper Flanagan and Nolan James Jr. give Carr plenty of other places to go.

The 6-foot-2 Gilbert in particular has been outstanding. FBS-only Game on Paper data has him at 13 catches for 251 yards on 15 targets, with 1.35 EPA per reception and an 80% success rate, while Faison is at 173 yards with 0.70 EPA per play. UNC cannot simply sell out against one receiver, which is what makes the matchup with Carr dangerous: even if Carolina takes Gilbert away for stretches, Faison is capable of producing the same problem.

Carolina’s secondary has done some good things, allowing just 6.0 yards per pass attempt, which is the reason for some optimism. It looked horrendous against Clemson, though: Jaiden Patterson had by far his worst game as a starter, and moving Peyton Waters from safety to cornerback to shut down Clemson’s bigger receivers was a gamble that did not pay off.

UNC does not need to completely shut down Notre Dame’s passing attack; it needs enough negative plays and incompletions to stop drives. Greg Smith already has two interceptions, and Notre Dame has not been immune to mistakes.

The Tar Heels are much less proven outside Jordan Shipp and Jelani Thurman. Thurman leads UNC with 183 receiving yards and Shipp has 164, while suspended wide receiver CJ Sadler has 73 in just two games, Trech Kekahuna has 71 and Mason Humphrey has 38.

The advanced numbers tell a different story. In FBS-only games, Shipp has caught 11 of 15 targets for 139 yards, with +0.85 EPA per play and a 67% success rate. Thurman has seven catches on 17 targets for 112 yards, at -0.22 EPA per play and a 29% success rate. The raw numbers make Thurman look like the clear number one target, but Shipp has been the more productive receiver on a per-play basis.

That comes down to playstyle. Shipp is a possession receiver with steady hands and the ability to move the chains, whilst Thurman is more of a game-breaking receiving tight end who can move across the line of scrimmage, be used in motion and run smooth, steady routes. In my opinion, the Tar Heels need to get Shipp involved early, and find ways to get Thurman into space rather than repeatedly asking him to win difficult downfield throws against tight coverage.

The possible inclusion of Jordan Washington would give UNC an experienced inline tight end who proved his blocking ability in a great fall camp. His power routes and ability to chip edges would be invaluable to a team desperate for help in pass protection, and would let Thurman be used more in the passing game.

CJ Sadler, the talented freshman who was the number one player in Michigan last year, has been out with a suspension after racking up numerous speeding-related offences. He has been training with the team over the bye week and is a possibility to play. My current expectation is that he won’t, but that may very well be wrong.

The lack of explosiveness in the passing game is a concern. Carolina’s offensive explosiveness rating is 1.04, ranked 135th nationally, which explains a lot of what happened at Clemson: the run game can move the ball, but the offence still needs a way to produce more chunk gains through the air.

This is where the matchup is much less hopeless than the overall numbers suggest. UNC’s defence has been significantly better than the offence, holding opponents to 4.6 yards per play, and it is ranked fifth nationally in defensive explosiveness, which I find very important.

Notre Dame’s offence has been very efficient, but not explosive: its offensive explosiveness rating is 1.11, ranked 124th nationally. The Irish win through consistency, staying ahead of the chains, forcing mistakes and hitting big plays when the opportunity appears rather than chasing them.

That gives Carolina a clearer defensive assignment than simply trying to survive 70 snaps: make Notre Dame drive the length of the field. Carr is too good to assume he will make mistakes on his own, but if UNC can limit the running game enough to create second-and-long, it reaches the part of the game where its pass rush can actually affect him. That is where Xavier Lewis, Jaylen Harvey and Melkart Abou-Jaoude (if fit) become critical.

The Carr problem

There is one thing UNC cannot completely eliminate: CJ Carr, who is playing excellent football, with nine touchdowns to one interception through four games and better than 10 yards per attempt. In my opinion, the best version of this Carolina defence is the one that makes him work for everything, rather than trying to completely shut him down.

They can do that by showing him different pre-snap coverage looks, mixing in blitzes and simulated pressure without telegraphing it, and trusting the secondary enough to send an extra rusher when down and distance allow. The biggest mistake would be playing scared and giving Carr easy completions underneath all afternoon.

Where UNC can attack Notre Dame

1. Passing downs

This is the biggest statistical opening. UNC does not necessarily need to dominate first down, but it needs to win enough first-down snaps to create obvious passing situations.

2. Get the ball to Jordan Shipp

Shipp has been one of UNC’s most efficient offensive players in FBS play, and he needs to be a central part of the game plan.

3. Use Hall without becoming predictable

The goal is not 30 carries; it is making Notre Dame respect him. If Hall is getting four or five yards on early downs, that creates the play-action opportunities UNC needs.

Where UNC needs to be concerned

The passing game

This remains the biggest issue. UNC’s offence is not disastrous, but it is nowhere near Notre Dame’s level, and against a defence with a 17% havoc rate, a couple of three-and-outs or a single negative play can change the game.

Third down

UNC is converting 38.2% of third downs to Notre Dame’s 41.5%. The bigger problem is on the defensive side, where UNC is allowing opponents to convert 44.4% of third downs, compared with just 35.2% for Notre Dame.

Turnovers

Notre Dame has a +2.25 turnover margin per game in the current advanced-stat data, compared with +0.50 for UNC. The Irish have already scored a defensive touchdown this season, and UNC cannot give them short fields.

Red zone finishing

UNC has already shown against Clemson that moving the ball into scoring position is not enough. The Tar Heels got inside the Clemson seven while leading 17-15 and ultimately settled for a field goal after the weather delay, and the defence then gave up the lead late. Field goals probably will not be enough against Notre Dame if Carolina is giving up short fields and touchdowns on the other side. Petrino and Edwards must sort out their red zone woes to give UNC a shot at a victory on Saturday afternoon.

Special teams

This is not likely to decide the game by itself, but UNC cannot afford to lose hidden yardage. Aeron Burrell is averaging 46.4 yards per punt, but he is just 4-for-7 on field goals this season, and his 47-yard miss against Clemson came after UNC’s defence had created a short field with a turnover. That kind of missed opportunity matters much more against a team like Notre Dame. The return of Rece Verhoff, who was recently ruled eligible, should ease the concern, but will he be rusty? We will have to wait and see.

The Irish are disciplined (2.5 penalties per game, one of the lowest figures in the country) and have been excellent at converting scoring opportunities. UNC has to take every available point.

What the game probably looks like if UNC is playing well

The ideal UNC game does not involve trying to match Notre Dame score for score. It starts with the defence controlling the early downs and forcing the Irish into second-and-long and third-and-long. That gives the pass rush a much better opportunity to get after CJ Carr and forces him to throw into tighter windows, which is where Xavier Lewis and the rest of the defensive front can have the biggest impact.

On offence, UNC needs to keep leaning on Hall, June and Edwards’ ability to create yards on the ground without becoming one-dimensional, mixing in higher-percentage throws and using the run to set up play-action. Edwards needs to play within the structure of the offence while using his legs when the opportunity arises, and Shipp needs to become a consistent part of the passing game. The biggest thing to avoid is the empty possession that changes a game: a three-and-out right after a strong defensive stop, or a turnover that gives Notre Dame the ball inside UNC territory.

Ultimately, UNC has to make this a long game. The longer it stays within one score, the more pressure moves onto Notre Dame and the more important each possession becomes. That is why this should not be viewed as simply 42.3 points per game against 23.3: the real question is whether UNC can force Notre Dame to play a kind of football it has not had to play much this season, with long drives, third-and-long situations and tight coverage.

What would concern me early

The first quarter will tell us a lot. If Notre Dame is running for five or six yards a pop and Carr is comfortable on first down, UNC’s defence will be under serious pressure, because easy early yards let the Irish stay ahead of the chains and keep the whole playbook available. If Hall is consistently getting hit behind the line of scrimmage, that is an early sign Notre Dame’s front is winning.

The same applies on the other side. If Edwards is repeatedly facing third-and-eight through third-and-10, the game is moving exactly where Notre Dame wants it. The opposite is a much better sign: UNC running efficiently, Edwards completing easy throws and the Irish facing third-and-seven or longer.

The biggest individual matchup

Xavier Lewis vs. Notre Dame's offensive line

There are plenty of major players in this game, but Xavier Lewis might be the one who can physically change the matchup. Six sacks through three games is outstanding production, and adding 8.5 tackles for loss shows he is not just getting home occasionally. He recorded two sacks against Clemson and has shown he can win both from the interior and from more aggressive alignments.

Notre Dame has protected Carr well, which is exactly why UNC needs Lewis to disrupt that protection. He should be moved around, including inside on obvious passing downs, and used in stunts rather than facing the same double team all afternoon. A tackle for loss on first down, a pressure that forces Carr off his spot or a second-down sack can change how a drive develops, and if Lewis is consistently in the backfield, Notre Dame will struggle to stay ahead of the chains.

The most important matchup on the other side: Billy Edwards Jr. vs. Notre Dame's pass rush

This is the harder matchup for Carolina. Edwards does not need to throw for 350 yards, but he does need to protect the football, stay on schedule and make enough throws to stop Notre Dame simply loading the box against the run. Against a defence allowing just 8.3 points per game, that is going to be difficult.

Carolina’s offence cannot spend the afternoon waiting for Notre Dame to make a mistake. It needs to manufacture positive plays and keep Edwards out of obvious passing situations through the running game, quick throws, play-action and his mobility. The first few possessions matter, as UNC needs to establish rhythm before Notre Dame starts dictating terms.

The advanced-stat view

The overall advanced numbers are heavily tilted towards Notre Dame, which is hardly surprising. One current opponent-adjusted model has the Irish at a 28.1 SP+ rating compared with 3.0 for UNC, with Notre Dame’s offence rated at 40.5 and its defence at 12.7, against 23.9 and 21.2 for UNC.

The rushing numbers are more encouraging for UNC. The Tar Heels are producing +0.124 rushing EPA per play, not far behind Notre Dame’s +0.142, which suggests there is a realistic way for Carolina to move the ball. But Notre Dame has been much more effective at stopping the run, allowing -0.229 rushing EPA per play compared with +0.058 for UNC.

What UNC cannot afford to do

UNC cannot turn this into a track meet. Notre Dame has too much efficiency and firepower for Carolina to spend the afternoon answering every score with another score. The Tar Heels need to be comfortable playing a more controlled game and trusting their defence to keep the Irish from creating separation.

Carolina also cannot abandon Hall because the first few carries go nowhere. UNC still needs to make the Irish defend the run for four quarters. But the answer cannot be throwing 35 times because Notre Dame is loading the box, either. Edwards needs enough help from the run game and the scheme to have manageable situations.

Final thoughts

Notre Dame is the more complete team right now, and the numbers make that pretty clear. The Irish have the more efficient quarterback, the more dangerous passing game, the stronger defence and considerably more margin for error.

That does not mean UNC has nothing to work with. The Tar Heels have a legitimate running game, a defensive line capable of creating havoc and a defence that has been far more consistent than the offence, and they showed for large stretches against Clemson that they can physically compete with a good Power Four opponent.

The problem is that Clemson also showed exactly where the margin for error is for this team. Carolina cannot afford to spend three quarters waiting for the offence to find a rhythm; against Notre Dame, it needs to be efficient from the start, make Carr work for his throws and consistently win on early downs. The red zone is part of that. UNC did not turn enough opportunities into touchdowns against Clemson, and when Carolina gets inside the Notre Dame 20, it needs to finish those drives.

The good news is that UNC does not need to completely reinvent itself for this matchup. The run game works, the defence has played well and the pass rush has the ability to create problems. The challenge is putting all of those things together for four quarters against the best opponent on the schedule to this point. That is what Saturday at Kenan Stadium is going to test.
